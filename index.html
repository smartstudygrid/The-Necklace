The Necklace

Guy de Maupassant  ·  Summary & Study Notes

Quick Summary

Mathilde Loisel is pretty and charming but married to a modest clerk, and she is miserable about her ordinary life. When her husband gets an invitation to a grand ministry ball, she is upset that she has nothing to wear. He gives her his savings for a gown, and she borrows a diamond necklace from her rich friend Madame Forestier. At the ball she is a huge success, but afterwards she realises she has lost the necklace.

Too afraid to confess, the Loisels buy an identical replacement for 36,000 francs, borrowing heavily. They spend ten years in poverty to repay the debt. Mathilde becomes old and rough. When she finally tells Madame Forestier the truth, she learns the original necklace was paste (fake), worth at most 500 francs.

Characters

Protagonist

Mathilde Loisel

Beautiful, vain and dissatisfied; dreams of luxury. After the loss she shows "sudden heroism" and works hard for ten years.
Husband

M. Loisel

A modest clerk at the Ministry of Public Instruction. Kind, practical and devoted; content with simple things (the "good soup"). Sacrifices his savings and his evenings.
Friend

Madame Forestier (Jeanne)

Mathilde's rich former convent schoolmate. Generous with the loan, but cool when the necklace comes back late. Her necklace turns out to be fake.
Minor

The Minister & Madame Ramponneau

Hosts of the ball. The minister notices Mathilde. The jeweler at the Palais Royal sells the replacement.

Plot Timeline

  • 1. Dissatisfaction. Mathilde, born into a family of clerks, hates her plain home and dreams of luxury. She avoids her rich friend because visiting makes her sad.
  • 2. The invitation. Loisel proudly brings an invitation to the Minister's ball. Mathilde is angry and cries: she has nothing to wear.
  • 3. The gown. Loisel gives her 400 francs, the money he saved for a gun. She still complains she has no jewels.
  • 4. The loan. Following her husband's idea, she borrows jewels from Madame Forestier and chooses a diamond necklace from a black satin box.
  • 5. The ball. Mathilde is the most admired woman there and even notices the minister's attention. Loisel sleeps in an anteroom. They leave at 4 a.m.
  • 6. The loss. At home she discovers the necklace is gone. Loisel searches the route, police, newspapers and cab companies, with no result.
  • 7. The cover-up. They tell Madame Forestier the clasp is being mended to buy time. After a week they give up hope.
  • 8. The replacement. They find a matching necklace at the Palais Royal for 36,000 francs. Loisel uses his inheritance and takes ruinous loans.
  • 9. The return. Madame Forestier coldly says it should have been returned sooner, but does not open the case.
  • 10. Ten years of poverty. They dismiss the servant, move to a garret, and Mathilde does heavy housework. Loisel works nights copying and doing accounts.
  • 11. The meeting. On the Champs-Elysees Mathilde, now aged and rough, confesses everything to Madame Forestier.
  • 12. The twist. Madame Forestier reveals the original necklace was paste, worth at most 500 francs.
The twist: All the sacrifice and ten lost years were for a worthless imitation. This is situational irony: the outcome is the opposite of what Mathilde (and the reader) expected.

Themes

Appearance vs. reality

The fake necklace looks real; Mathilde looks rich at the ball but is not.

Vanity & materialism

Mathilde's desire for status and luxury leads directly to her ruin.

Pride & honesty

Fear and pride stop her from admitting the loss, turning a small problem into a decade of debt.

Fate & chance

"How small a thing is needed to make or ruin us!" Life can change on one small event.

Class & social rank

She feels she was born for a higher station; money and clothing define worth in her world.

Sacrifice & endurance

The couple face hardship with dignity; hardship changes Mathilde's character and appearance.

Symbols & Literary Devices

The necklaceFalse value, vanity, illusion of wealth; the cause of her downfall.
Paste jewelsSurface beauty without real worth; mirrors Mathilde's own false pride.
The gown & wrapsLuxury vs. poverty; the "modest wraps of common life" make her feel exposed.
The soup tureenLoisel's simple contentment, in contrast with Mathilde's fantasies.
IronySituational (the necklace was fake) and dramatic (Mathilde could have asked first).
ForeshadowingThe "slip of fate" in the opening hints that fate will turn on her.
Third-person narrationOmniscient narrator reveals Mathilde's thoughts and judges her vanity.

Vocabulary & Setting

Crossly

In an annoyed, irritable way (Mathilde throws down the invitation).

Paste

Glass used to imitate gems; fake jewelry.

Usurers

Lenders who charge extremely high interest.

Dowry

Money or property a woman brings to her marriage.

Setting

Paris, France, late 19th century. Places: Rue des Martyrs, the Ministry palace, Palais Royal, the Seine quay, Champs-Elysees.

Key Quotes

"Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste!"
"How small a thing is needed to make or ruin us!"

Remember for Exams

  • Mathilde wants status, borrows, loses, replaces, repays (cause and effect chain).
  • The ending is ironic and sudden; discuss how the twist changes our view of the whole story.
  • Contrast Mathilde (never satisfied) with her husband (kind and content).
  • Ten years of hardship change her from a vain beauty to a hard, strong woman. Does she gain anything?
  • The story's moral: honesty and contentment matter more than appearances.
